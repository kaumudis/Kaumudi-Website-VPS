Juni: Accounting Spotted accounting as a revenue and adoption opportunity for banks, and turned SME finance teams into our champions.

Why

Juni’s banking products were live, but adoption lagged. Accountants told customers that Juni made month-end harder, so the people with the most influence weren’t on our side.

Juni also needed revenue beyond card and credit fees. I saw one answer to both problems.

What

  • Went to the source. I mapped month-end with accountants and finance teams. The work sat between the bank and the books: re-keying, chasing receipts, coding every line, exporting by hand.
  • Made the bet. If Juni owned that gap, it would become part of the finance team’s daily work, which is what makes customers stay and pay.
  • Went deep, not wide. One deep, two-way ERP integration, designed with our senior engineers, so the books close and balance automatically.
  • Built it AI-first. Receipts matched automatically, account and VAT codes suggested, transactions sent to the ERP in one click, supplier invoices paid from Juni.

Result

90% adoption among eligible customers. 60–70% of manual month-end work removed.

SME finance teams became our champions: the accounting module saved them time and let business owners do what they do best, which is sell. Accounting also became the foundation for Juni’s SaaS pricing.

Today, accounting automation is a category start-ups are built around.

Juni
Juni: AI in finance workflows Businesses asked for rules. We gave them AI that takes the manual work out of running finance operations, built with the data team.

Why

After accounting launched, month-end was still full of repeat work: chasing receipts, typing in invoices, coding every line. Accountants kept asking us for rules to automate it. Our data team had an idea for AI that could, but no path to ship it.

Rules were the automation customers knew. What they needed was less manual work.

What

  • Showed, then asked. Engineers from my team built a proof of concept with the data team before we asked for roadmap time. The founders backed it, and I reserved capacity for data work every cycle after that.
  • Gave the model the right data. Product supplied labelled data from customers’ own history, so the AI learned how each business actually works.
  • Designed by confidence. Confident predictions are filled in and marked as AI. Less confident ones show as suggestions with alternatives, and a person confirms.
  • Built a learning loop. Every correction goes back into the model, and the data team finds the root cause of every miss.
  • Designed for the misses. A dedicated place to match receipts by hand, careful “suggested” wording, and regular updates on the hours AI saved.
  • Expanded step by step. Receipts, then account coding, then invoices by email, then line items split across several entries.

Result

AI now works on every transaction, with no setup. What shipped: receipts matched automatically from email and from 10,000+ partner stores, one card payment split into several line items, AI that reads supplier invoices, and suggested account and VAT codes.

Accounting partners call it “a game changer for our clients”, and AI became how Juni describes itself: “Where banking meets accounting. Powered by AI.”

Lesson

Even a small share of misses created support tickets, because customers expected AI to be perfect. Accuracy isn’t the product; trust is. Design for the misses from day one.

Agentic Finance Lab Live I built this lab to show that agentic finance is here to stay, and will transform how we move money, buy and shop.

Why I built it

Agentic finance isn’t the future. It’s here, and product teams need to build differently for it. I built a working finance agent to show how.

Most teams are adding AI to finance products the same way they add any feature, and that isn’t safe. This lab shows what the right design looks like, end to end.

How it works

Architecture

  1. TelegramI ask: “Is INV-1005 ready to pay?”
  2. Hermes agentUnderstands the question, calls tools, explains
  3. MCPFour narrow, read-only tools
  4. Finance APIFixed rules → ready, blocked or needs review
  5. PostgreSQLFictional invoices, suppliers and accounts

Docker on my own server · no real money

  • Telegram is the interface. A finance user asks about an invoice in plain language.
  • The agent (Hermes) understands the question, picks the right tool and explains the answer. It never makes the payment decision itself.
  • MCP gives the agent four narrow tools: list invoices, get an invoice, get a supplier and assess payment readiness. None of them can change data or move money.
  • The Finance API owns the decision. Fixed rules check invoice approval, the supplier, verified bank details, currency, the payment account and the available balance, then return ready, blocked or needs review, with a reason code and the evidence.
  • PostgreSQL holds fictional invoices, suppliers and accounts. Only the API can read it.

An example

Asked “Is INV-1005 ready for payment?”, the agent called the readiness tool and replied: blocked, INSUFFICIENT_AVAILABLE_BALANCE. The invoice was approved, but £25,000 was due against £20,000 available. A model alone might have said “approved, so ready”; the rules caught it.

Design decisions

  • The API is the source of truth; the agent can’t invent or override financial facts.
  • The agent never touches the database; everything goes through the API’s controls.
  • Every answer uses fresh evidence; conversation memory is never trusted for money questions.
  • A human approves every payment until the agent has earned more authority.

What I did

  • Turned every surprise into a recorded decision. When the agent behaved in a way I hadn’t planned for, I wrote down what we’d decide, why, and what it meant next. Fifteen decisions now shape the design.
  • Separated judgement from rules. Payment checks live in code as fixed rules. The agent understands the question, calls the rules and explains the outcome; within those limits it uses its own judgement. Instructions guide; code enforces.
  • Gave the agent the least access it needs. It can only look things up through an API over MCP. It has no direct database access and no way to move money, and all data is fictional.
  • Made every answer checkable. Each reply comes back as ready, blocked or needs review, with a reason code and the evidence, so a finance person can verify it in seconds.
  • Measured it with evals. Test scenarios with the expected outcome for each, re-run after every change to catch regressions.
  • Planned the path to real money. Next come approvals by a second person and the new agent payment protocols, such as Google’s AP2, so payments only move with the right sign-off.

Result so far

Deterministic rules and evals around the agent keep its financial decisions sound and correct.

Product framework for agentic finance

What product teams must change to build agentic finance Live Seven rules I now give anyone building agents that handle money, each backed by what the lab showed.

“A correct answer can hide a failed process.”

The rules

  1. Decide who owns the decision. Access to financial data doesn’t give a model the right to make a financial decision. Name the system that owns the facts and the one that decides. In the lab, the agent drew its own sensible conclusion before any rule existed; that’s exactly what you don’t want in production.
  2. Put the rules in code, not in the prompt. Important financial rules must give the same answer to the same inputs, every time. The model explains the rule; it doesn’t invent it.
  3. Give agents narrow tools, not broad access. One tool, one clear job, with a contract that says what it can’t do. Narrow tools are easier to test and harder to misuse.
  4. Ground every answer in fresh evidence. Giving an agent a trusted source doesn’t mean it will use it. Told to answer from memory, my agent did.
  5. Test the whole journey, not just the answer. Check the request, the tool chosen, the evidence, the decision and the reply. My agent gave the right answer while skipping the required check; only the logs showed it.
  6. Make it auditable. An agent saying it used a tool isn’t proof. Keep independent records of every request, call and decision.
  7. Let autonomy be earned. Explain, then recommend, then prepare a draft, then act after human approval, then act within a set mandate. Each step only once the evidence supports it.

What this means for product teams

Designing an agent is more than designing a user experience. Product teams now have to design the agent’s context, tools, authority, decision boundaries, behaviour when it’s unsure, and how it recovers. Separate the model’s judgement from deterministic controls, evaluate the full path, and expand autonomy step by step as trust is proven.

Value, trust, reach

Go-to-market for AI products Three things every AI launch has to get right, how to size each launch, and how to roll it out.

Three pillars

  • Value. One workflow done brilliantly, with a clear before and after. Outcomes the customer can measure, not vanity metrics. A story for each segment, told in that customer's own words.
  • Trust. Governance, accuracy and transparency are part of the pitch. Controls and being able to see what the AI did become reasons to choose you. Pricing covers what each customer actually costs to serve.
  • Reach. Launches sized to what they change. Short, ongoing sales enablement instead of one big training. Spread through people customers already trust, backed by hard proof.

When anyone can build quickly, the advantage goes to whoever launches well.

Size the launch

Shipping is not launching. Releases can be grouped, held back or combined so customers hear one clear story.

  • Big: a new product or a new category. A full campaign, press and leadership involved.
  • Medium: a major capability or integration. A targeted campaign, a webinar and sales enablement.
  • Small: an improvement. A changelog entry, an in-app message and an email.

Choose how it lands

  • One big moment: everything announced at once. Highest risk and highest reward; for the big launches only.
  • A drumbeat: a series of announcements that builds interest over several weeks.
  • In stages: beta, then limited release, then everyone, moving on only when usage, satisfaction and incident rates say it's ready.

Ways to limit who gets it first: release by country, invite customers to opt in early, or build it with a small group of design partners.

Why AI launches stall

Six reasons AI launches stall What gets in the way when you take an AI product to market, and what to plan for before launch day.
  1. Expected, but not paid for. Customers now assume AI comes included, and few companies have found a way to charge for it.
  2. Costs that grow with use. Every AI action costs money to run, so a popular feature on a flat subscription can quietly eat the margin.
  3. Everyone sounds the same. When every product says “AI-powered”, the phrase stops setting anyone apart.
  4. Trust comes first. Buyers hold back over how their data is used, wrong answers and compliance.
  5. Interest isn’t intent. A great demo and a wave of trials can hide the fact that people won’t keep using it or pay for it.
  6. The rest of the company can’t keep up. Sales, support, marketing and legal fall behind how fast product now ships.
